Elevated methane concentrations are observed in the shallow water column above subsea permafrost
on the East Siberian Arctic Shelf, including the inner Laptev Sea. The subsea source of this methane is
poorly understood, yet crucial for predicting future methane emissions. Here, we combine analyses of
dissolved methane concentrations, triple-isotopic ﬁngerprinting, and Bayesian Markov Chain Monte
Carlo statistics to constrain the source of high-concentration methane sampled during four
expeditions, 2016-2020. In contrast to earlier ﬁndings of predominantly thermogenic methane release
in the outer Laptev Sea, our results for the major methane release region of the inner Laptev Sea
suggest that old microbial methane (radiocarbon age >48000 y BP) is being released from preformed
methane pools stored within the subsea permafrost system. This observation reveals that several
sources contribute to the elevated methane on the shelf and thus the necessity to consider a diversity
of sources when estimating future methane release trajectories.
Highly elevated levels of methane (CH4) have been detected across
extensive scales of the East Siberian Arctic Shelf (ESAS) over the past two
decades1–3. Next to enriched CH4 concentrations in both seawater and
the atmospheric boundary layer, strong ebullition was observed at several
seep sites across the ESAS3–5. The ESAS (<200 m depth) is the world’s
largest shelf sea (~1.7 × 106 km2), yet it is very shallow with an average
depth of only ~45 m6. It started forming ~15 ky before present (BP) as the
sea level rose, inundating Pleistocene-age terrestrial permafrost7–10. The
resulting subsea permafrost below the ESAS is estimated to stretch over
~1.5 × 106 km2 at an average thickness of ~330 m11,12. Based on subsea
drill coring through the upper horizon of the subsea permafrost, repeated
21–22 years apart, it was shown that subsea permafrost is now thawing
~35 times faster than nearby terrestrial permafrost10,13. The thawing
subsea permafrost contains preformed CH4 and organic matter (OM)
that could contribute to the observed intense CH4 emissions. However,
the CH4 sources releasing CH4 to the ESAS seawater are poorly con-
strained. Constraining the relative importance of the different CH4
sources is a prerequisite for predicting to what extent the observed CH4
emissions in the ESAS will intensify in the future.
Several old subsea pools could contribute to the elevated CH4 levels on
the ESAS. The deepestsubseaCH4 sources are fossilnaturalgasreservoirsof
either microbial or thermogenic origin, formed below the permafrost
layer14,15. The amount of CH4 stored in such reservoirs is uncertain but may
be ~1–5 GtC for the ESAS16–18, i.e., respectively ~3–21% and ~1–7% of
Russia’s natural gas reserves and resources19. The overlying subsea perma-
frostalsoholdssubstantialamountsofcarbonintheformofbothpreformed
CH4andasOMthatcanbemicrobiallydegradedtoCH4uponthaw13,20.The
preformed CH4 within the subsea permafrost layer could originate from
past in situ methanogenesis21,22. Furthermore, upward-moving gases from
sub-permafrostfossilgasreservoirscould gettrapped within23orbelow14the
subsea permafrost layer.
The amounts of both OM and CH4 in subsea permafrost reservoirs are
highly uncertain. Estimates of the amount of OM in subsea permafrost are
~2800 GtC for the circum-Arctic24 and ~500 GtC for the ESAS2. The CH4
trapped by subsea permafrost may be present as free gas but also as gas
hydrate, a solid where water (H2O) and CH4 molecules combine to a
crystalline structure25. A gas hydrate is stable within speciﬁc pressure-
temperature boundaries, where higher pressures and lower temperatures
favor the hydrate phase26. An overview of estimated amounts of preformed
CH4 stored in oceanic and (subsea) permafrost hydrates, as free gas/hydrate
within subsea permafrost, and in gas reserves/resources is provided in

There are multiple mechanisms that can release CH4 from the old
subsea CH4 and OM reservoirs. First, the combination of geothermal heat,
warm overlying Holocene seawater, and human-induced additional climate
warming results in subsea permafrost thawing1,4,10. Such warming may
induce microbial degradation of OM and concomitant CH4 production in
the thawed subsea permafrost, but also results in the direct release of subsea
permafrost-trapped CH4 by the destabilization of the subsea permafrost
itself 2,28–30. Existing and developing taliks, i.e., unfrozen layers/bodies in
permafrost31, can act as migration pathways for the release of subsea
permafrost-trapped CH4 to the water column2,10,29,32,33. Furthermore,
increasing subsea temperatures could destabilize CH4 hydrates26, releasing
CH4 while facilitating the upward transport of formerly trapped preformed
CH4 from greater depths. Finally, the ESAS is tectonically active34–37, and
CH4 originating from fossil gas reservoirs could seep along faults formed by
tectonic activity2,28,38,39.
In addition to these old subsea CH4 sources, several younger and/or
laterallytransportedsourcescanpotentiallyalsocontributeCH4tothewater
column. Holocene shelf sediments contain both laterally transported OM
(from coastal erosion or river discharge) and marine OM, which can be
microbially degraded to CH4 under anoxic conditions. Rivers32,40 and
potentiallysubmarinegroundwaterdischarge41cantransportdissolvedCH4
to the ESAS, although dissolved CH4 is largely reduced in concentration
during freshwater transport to the sea42.
The heterogeneity of the subsea geology and permafrost in the ESAS
precludes generalizing CH4 emissions to the water column and the atmo-
sphere based on the currently available data. While the origin of high CH4
concentrations at outer Laptev Sea hotspot locations was identiﬁed as pre-
dominantly stemming from fossil thermogenic gas3, the origin of CH4
released in otherregions of the ESAS remains largely unknown. Speciﬁcally,
the inner Laptev Sea has been found to hold extensive seeps4,10 and highly
elevated CH4 concentrations1,2,4, yet CH4 sources are poorly constrained.
Given its shallow depth (<25 m), there is a short conduit to the atmosphere
for any sediment-escaping CH4.
Several traits make permafrost underneath the inner Laptev Sea sus-
ceptible to destabilization. The region has been inundated comparatively
recently (starting ~10 ky BP7,8) and is suggested to have relicts of its ter-
restrial past in the form of taliks from paleo-rivers and thermokarst
processes10,11,29,43–45. This coastal sea also has more pronounced lateral inputs
than deeper offshore regions, including heat from the Lena River plume32.
Moreover, the few existing core-based measurements of shallow coastal
subsea permafrost indicate a thaw rate ~35 times greater than that of its
terrestrial counterpart10,13 and, in the upper 57 m, a temperature 8–11 °C
higher4. This suggests that the shallow subsea permafrost is much more
vulnerable than nearby terrestrial permafrost, likely because of the long-
term heating from overlying seawater29,46. The geological setting, highly
elevated CH4 levels, and vulnerability of the inner Laptev Sea subsea per-
mafrost make this a key subregion to disentangle what sources are dom-
inating the CH4 releases in the ESAS.
The rapid and ampliﬁed warming of the Arctic additionally motivates
the study of sources and release mechanisms of CH4 in the inner Laptev Sea.
The Arctic is warming three to four times faster than the global average47,48,
and a warmer climate would likely accelerate CH4 release in the ESAS
region. Benthic seawater temperatures in the shallow ESAS (<25 m) have
increased substantially compared to those in the deeper parts of the shelf 29.
The observational record shows that mean summer bottom-water tem-
peratures increased by ~1 °C for the 1999–2009 decade compared to the
100-year mean4,29, a process which may have accelerated over the past
decade concomitant with wider Arctic warming trends. Potential effects of
rising temperatures are increased CH4 hydrate destabilization, increased
subsea permafrost destabilization, releasing subsea permafrost-trapped
CH4, increased OM degradation in thawing subsea permafrost, and
increased lateral inputs of OM and CH4. Some of these positive feedback
mechanisms to climate change may evolve gradually, while others may be
more abrupt. Future CH4 emissions from thawing (subsea) permafrost and
destabilizing CH4 hydrates are two of the large uncertainties in the changing
CH4 budget and are considered potential biogeochemical tipping points for
climate change49,50. In addition, the estimated size of the subsea OM and
CH4depositsintheESASis~1–4 × 102 timeslargerthanthetotalamountof
CH4 in the atmosphere (3.8 GtC)2,27,50. However, the current atmospheric
monitoring networks cannot detect a sudden multiple-year-long CH4
release event in the ESAS of the size of a 100% increased oceanic ﬂux51.
Improved constraints on the type and evolution of CH4 emissions in the
ESAS are necessary for reliable projections of this region’s current and
future, potentially intensifying CH4 emissions.

Results and discussion
Elevated methane concentrations and ubiquitous bubble plumes
in the inner Laptev Sea
High CH4 concentrations and many bubble plumes were observed in the
inner Laptev Sea during the four expeditions. A major hotspot region with
strongly elevated CH4 concentrations (up to 27,000 nM; Supplementary
Data 2–5) was repeatedly observed along with dense ebullition patterns
(Fig.2; Supplementary Note 3 and Supplementary Data 6). Northand south
of this major methane hotspot (MMH), CH4 concentrations were still up to
150 nM for samples retrieved during 2016–2020; all of these are sub-
stantially elevated compared to ~4 nM that would result if there were no
local source and just equilibration with atmospheric CH4.
There is some variation between years in spatial concentration gra-
dients and in the locations of observed CH4 hotspots. The MMH observed
in 2016–2020 was slightly northward compared to CH4 hotspots identiﬁed
earlier. In 2005, bottom water samples showed a distinct CH4 hotspot
101 kmawayfromtherecentMMH(Fig.2)2.In2007,CH4hotspotsbeneath
sea ice were observed 79–57 km away from this MMH1. In 2009 and 2010,
water column data showed a major CH4 hotspot about 68 km from the
MMH4. Varying locations of the most apparent CH4 hotspots over 15 years
indicate that multiple CH4 hotspots are present in the inner Laptev Sea and
possibly have a changing activity level over short time scales (years to
decades). The central impression is that there is a continuous ubiquity of
intensive CH4 hotspots in this inner Laptev Sea region.
At the MMH, dissolved CH4 concentrations were up to 6000 times
higher than expected from equilibrium with the atmosphere. The highest
CH4 concentrations were observed below the pycnocline (~10–15 m deep)
in the bottom water layer (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Note 2). The geo-
graphical alignment of the highest CH4 concentrations with the intensity of
the ebullition pattern at the MMH suggests partial dissolution from rising
bubbles as a key cause of the elevated seawater CH4 concentrations. These
combinedﬁndingspointtooneorseveralcontinuousseabedsourcesofCH4
causing the perennial and strongly elevated CH4 concentrations in the
shallow water column of the inner Laptev Sea.

The release of CH4 from ancient seabed sourcesto the bottomseawater
has been proposed for two other Arctic shelf sea regions; however, in both
cases, the generated isotopic data were limited and based on single expe-
ditions. The reports for the Beaufort Sea did not feature hydrogen isotope
data and did not utilize the generated stable carbon isotope data for more
detailed source apportionment55. The identiﬁed ancient CH4 was suggested
to originate from CH4 hydrates, permafrost, and/or geologic sources55. For
the outer Laptev Sea, very few Δ14CCH4 data were generated3. These were
combined with single-year dual-stable isotope data, and the release of
thermogenic CH4 was suggested3.
In addition to constraining the old characteristics of the strong CH4
emissionsource,theΔ14CCH4dataintheKeelingplotsalsoyieldinformation
on the character/source of the CH4 pool of lower concentration that it is
mixing into. The consistently higher Δ14CCH4 values associated with lower
CH4 concentrations observed here indicate a background of less-aged CH4,
which appears to be relatively constant over 2019–2020, considering the
good ﬁt in the Keeling plot for the years combined (Fig. 3B, R2 = 0.75). The
relatively younger background pool mixing with large amounts of old CH4

thawed subsea permafrost and/or in sediment layers accumulated during
the Holocene, or preformed CH4 transported by rivers. Any of these
younger CH4 sources deliver microbially produced CH4.

As Δ14C measurements are, by deﬁnition, corrected for isotopic
fractionation62 and thereby unaffected by microbial oxidation, the strong
Δ14CCH4 Keeling regressions (Fig. 3) inform about the source signature. The
good ﬁts of the regression lines, combined with slopes being evidently larger
than zero, support a scenario where a primary source mixes with a back-
ground of much lower CH4 concentration. Microbial degradation of CH4 in
the sediment or water column would result in a regression slope close to
zero. Hence, there is an overwhelmingly old source causing the highly
elevated CH4 concentrations, which is mixing with a background pool of
younger CH4. This supports the use of Keeling plots to interpret the stable
isotope data in a mixing scenario, as discussed next.

Dual-stable isotope signatures of dissolved methane
Multi-year dual-stable isotope results of dissolved CH4 in the inner Laptev
Sea indicate that microbially-produced CH4 is the primary source, and that
the source signature is very similar for all four studied years. The Keeling
plot-derived multi-year average source signatures (±1σ) for the below
pycnocline samples were −72 ± 2‰ for δ13CCH4 and −313 ± 19‰ for
δ2HCH4 (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 2). The multi-year average source
signature (±1σ) using the above pycnocline samples was very similar at
−73 ± 3‰ for δ13CCH4, and −314‰ for δ2HCH4 in 2020 (Fig. 4 and Sup-
plementary Table 2). These low stable carbon and hydrogen isotope values
pointtoprimarymicrobiallyformedCH4
53,54.Dependingonthepathwayby
which CH4 is released to the water column (bubble dissolution versus dif-
fusion from the sediment), CH4 may be susceptible to anaerobic CH4 oxi-
dation during its sedimentary transit, altering its stable isotope
composition54. The observed dual-stable isotope source signature is, how-
ever, very constant between years (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 2) and
ebullition is ubiquitously observed (Fig. 2). Therefore, bubble dissolution
likely represents the dominant pathway for CH4 addition to the water
column. Hence, anaerobic oxidation of CH4 in the sediments would, if
present, only marginally affect the observed dual-stable isotope source
signatures.
The observed source signature for the inner Laptev Sea is in substantial
contrast to observations for the outer Laptev Sea, where source signatures of
−43‰ to −55‰ for δ13CCH4 and −137‰ to −158‰ for δ2HCH4 were
identiﬁed, and consequently a thermogenic origin of CH4 was suggested for
that region ~350 km offshore3. Combining the stable isotope and Δ14CCH4
data from the inner Laptev Sea indicates a predominantly old microbial
source, in our source compartment terms SPAM and/or FOPRIM, being
released into the inner Laptev Sea seawater at the MMH, which contrasts
with the thermogenic FOTSEM seeps in the outer Laptev Sea.

Putative release pathways
We deduce that the released SPAM originates predominantly from a pre-
formed pool instead of being freshly produced in thawing subsea perma-
frost. This is supported by the ubiquity of gas bubble ﬁelds in the hotspot
regions (Fig. 2). Recent modeling studies are inconclusive regarding the
contribution of OM degradation in thawing subsea permafrost to the
observed CH4 emissions in the ESAS. One modeling study of subsea per-
mafrost OC stocks proposes low microbial decomposition rates of OM in
thawing subsea permafrost (~0.13 mmol C m−2 d−1)24. In contrast, another
model suggests substantial CH4 production rates from OM degradation in
thawed subsea permafrost (~11 mmol CH4 m−2 d−1)63. Extrapolations of
incubation experiments using subsea permafrost from the inner Laptev Sea
show a minor in situ microbial CH4 production rate of only 0.133
(σ = 0.167) mmol CH4 m−2 d−113. This was the rate estimated for a thawed
layer of 14 ± 7 m thickness, i.e., the average thaw depth in 2012–2014 of
eightsubseapermafrostcoresfromtheinnerLaptevSea13.Suchalimitedgas
release rate cannot explain the large bubble ﬂuxes observed with the echo-
sounder at the MMH (Fig. 2; Supplementary Note 3 and Supplementary
Data 6) nor the observation-based CH4 ebullition ﬂuxes estimated for this
region of 18 (6.2–39) mmol CH4 m−2 d−14. We conclude that the released
SPAM is dominantly not recently produced but more likely released from a
preformed pool of free gas or destabilizing CH4 hydrate within subsea
permafrost. An implication is that the observed CH4 releases might not
necessarily develop gradually but may be more abrupt in nature.
Estimates of CH4 hydrate abundance on the ESAS vary widely (Sup-
plementary Table 1). Gas hydrates form under speciﬁc conditions when
CH4 and H2O are situated together at a speciﬁc ratio, pressure, and
temperature25. The pressure and temperature conditions needed to form a
CH4 hydraterestrict CH4 hydrate formation to the polar regions and deeper
(>~300 m) oceans26,64. Shallow CH4 hydrates can be formed at ~200 m
depth in permafrost (~170–220 m at ~−15 to −5 °C)26, and hydrates have
been identiﬁed in Arctic permafrost65–67. Yet, the gas hydrate stability region
may shift upwards/downwards in the presence of other gases, such as car-
bon dioxide or longer hydrocarbons, and ionic compounds like NaCl26.
Furthermore, relict CH4 hydrates formed by ice sheet-induced pressures
during glaciations and subsequently retained outside the hydrate stability
zone by self-preservation66,68 could be present even shallower in
permafrost69,70.However, the lack of ice sheetsin the innerLaptev Sea region
duringtheLastGlacialMaximum71–73likelyprohibitsthegrowthofhydrates
formed due to glacier-exerted pressure. Some modeling studies estimate an
upperborderofthe gashydratestabilityzoneinthe ESAS at~13045 and ~75
m46 during the Pleistocene, while currently at ~200 m45,46. Reported subsea
permafrostdrillcoresfromtheESASextend to~50 mdepth4,10,i.e.,notdeep
enough to verify the presence of hydrates in the ESAS region. All in all, the
abundance of CH4 hydrates on the ESAS and their associated depths is
uncertain.

Taliks of different origin may act as conduits for upward-moving CH4.
TheinnerLaptevSeaistectonicallyactive,andthestudyareaislocatedinthe
Ust’-Lensky rift basin, which contains many faults29,34,35,78. Such faults and
the associated taliks from penetrating geothermal heat46,79 are a potential
pathway for the release of SPAM. Other types of taliks, from paleo-river
channels or submerged thaw lakes, have also been reported to be present in
the inner Laptev Sea and could also act as vertical conduits for the release of
preformed CH4
10,29,32,80. Taliks formed by paleo-river channels have been
modeled as being able to explain gas venting from dissociating hydrate
deposits33. Warming from both the top and the bottom after submergence
was modeled to increase talik width, resulting in gas hydrate dissociation
near the talik edges, driving gas transport to the water column33. Similarly,
open taliks, acting as gas migration pathways for preformed subsea-
permafrost trapped CH4, have been modeled to be present over large areas
in the inner Laptev Sea4. Furthermore, sub-bottom proﬁle images for the
coastal Laptev Sea showed blanked areas interpreted as propagating gas
plumes co-located with a submerged lake basin10. The overlying bubble
plumes in the water column pointed to gas propagation through a sub-
merged thaw lake talik, and it was hypothesized that such submerged thaw
lake taliks develop further downwards after inundation10,29,81. Further
mechanisms for talik formation have also been observed. On the Beaufort
Seashelf, gas ﬂares were observed to be associated with pingo-like features82.
These were interpreted as being formed by upward sediment extrusion due
to sub-permafrost gas pressure build-up from gas hydrate decomposition82,
an example of gas release and talik formation driven by geothermal heat
from below. In summary, existing and developing taliks of different origins
could explain the observed release of SPAM in the inner Laptev Sea.
At the major CH4 hotspot in our study area, we have identiﬁed SPAM
as the primary source, while fractions of FOPRIM and FOTSEM gases are
also suggested (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Note 8). The release of CH4 along
taliks from subsea permafrost-trapped gas, potentially from dissociating
CH4 hydrates, aligns with these observations as SPAM could have mixed
with FOPRIM and FOTSEM gases while being trapped. Furthermore, the
demonstrated presence of taliks in the inner Laptev Sea and the propagation
of gas plumesthrough theseabed2,10,83make CH4 releasealongthesefeatures
plausible. Moreover, local ebullition hotspots at many locations in the inner
LaptevSea align with developing taliks releasing preformed CH4. Therefore,
we believe that the leakage of subsea permafrost-associated CH4 along
existing and/or developing taliks is the most likely explanation for the
observedintensive CH4releaseatthemajorCH4hotspotintheinnerLaptev
Sea. Taken all together, the observed CH4 is released from a preformed CH4
pool associated with degrading subsea permafrost.
The fate of methane after release from the seabed
The old microbial subsea permafrost-associated CH4, released at the major
CH4 hotspot in the inner Laptev Sea, is observed to mix with a younger
microbial, likely partly oxidized, CH4 background in the water column. All
samples below the pycnocline with dual-stable isotope data lie within stable
isotope regimes of CH4 formed by primary microbial pathways53,54 (Fig. 5).
This aligns with the proposed scenario in which CH4 is predominantly
released from an old microbial CH4 source, mixing into a younger (Fig. 3)
yet microbial CH4 background. The observed trend of enrichment in the
stable isotopes with decreasing concentration is consistent with a partly
oxidized CH4 background pool, considering the enriched stable isotopic
composition of the background material53,54 (Fig. 4). The different slopes of
the Keeling plots to either the north (74°N) or south (72°N) of the MMH
(Fig. 4) could point to mixing with background material having different
concentrations and/or more/less isotopically enriched CH4. The isotopic
enrichment could also partly originate from aerobic oxidation of the CH4
released at the MMH.However,the stableisotope Keelingplots show strong
linear relationships, and the δ2HCH4 data manifest as a cloud around
−300‰ across a range of CH4 concentrations at the MMH in 2020 (Fig. 4).
This suggests that CH4 oxidation is not an important cause of the varying
CH4 concentrations. Instead, CH4 from the same source appears to be
released/mixed into the water column to different extents, possibly through
bubble

Additional lines of evidence support that there is little microbial
CH4 oxidation of the primary source in the water column. The dual-
stable isotope plot (Fig. 5) shows a range of literature-based dual-stable
isotope fractionation lines derived from microbial incubation experi-
ments (Supplementary Table 3). These lines were derived by computing
the shift in δ13CCH4 for the CH4 fraction remaining corresponding to a
δ2HCH4 shift from −300‰ to −150‰ of the source material. The
resulting theoretical range of the dual-stable isotope fractionation lines
for microbial aerobic oxidation in the water column, i.e., a Δδ13C:Δδ2H
ratio of 7.5 ± 2.2 (±1σ), does not align with the ﬁtted Δδ13C:Δδ2H lines
through the measured dual-stable isotope data, as these were much lar-
ger, ranging from 13 to 24 (Fig. 5). This indicates that the change in stable
isotope composition in our seawater samples does not reﬂect CH4 oxi-
dation in the water column, but is more likely caused by mixing with a
background pool. Importantly, any dilution of the primary source
material and additional source addition, for example, by bubble dis-
solution, would keep the observed stable isotope data along the mixing
line. Finally, the bottom, mid-column, and surface waters at the MMH
show CH4 of similar stable isotopic composition (Fig. 7), which again
aligns with minor CH4 oxidation in the water column in this region.

Implications
Multi-year triple-isotopic analysis of seawater CH4 from the inner Laptev
SearegionwithintensivesedimentaryCH4emissionsindicatesthereleaseof
predominantly old, microbial CH4 formerly trapped in subsea permafrost.
Basedontheebullitivecharacterandthegeologicalsetting,itishypothesized
that(developing)taliksfacilitatetheseepageofthispreformedCH4,possibly
from destabilizing CH4 hydrates. Importantly, our results point towards the
release of preformed CH4 from degrading subsea permafrost, hereby pro-
viding insights on a vulnerable CH4 compartment in the context of climate
warming.
The major ﬁnding of old, microbial, and subsea permafrost-associated
CH4 as the primary source in the inner Laptev Sea contrasts with thermo-
genic CH4 releasedin the outer Laptev Sea. This indicatesthat multiple CH4
pools contribute to the observations of elevated CH4 concentrations on the
extensive ESAS, the largest shelf region of the world. This implies that
multiple sources and release pathways must be considered in estimates of
current and future CH4 releases from the ESAS system. Furthermore,
considering the size of the subsea permafrost-associated CH4 pool and its
vulnerability, this may be an intensifying natural CH4 release process that
may be one part of the poorly understood current increase in global
atmospheric CH4, where enhanced natural releases of biogenic CH4 are
suggested85,86. The identiﬁed CH4 release from subsea permafrost, a little-
studied yet major earth system compartment, is exacerbated by the inefﬁ-
cient removal through microbial oxidation in the water column, likely
yielding the majority of the CH4 being also released into the atmosphere.
The observed release of formerly subsea-permafrost-trapped CH4 in the
inner Laptev Sea is coupled to subsea permafrost destabilization, a process
that is expected to continue with global warming and needs further
consideration.
Methods
Expeditions and sampling stations
This study is based on four extensive expeditions to the distant ESAS. In
2016, the R/V Akademik M.A. Lavrentyev traversed the inner Laptev Sea at
the end of September while in October 2018, 2019, and 2020, the R/V
Akademik Mstislav Keldysh (AMK) crossed the inner Laptev Sea (Fig. 2).
Sampling was focused on regions where high CH4 concentrations had been
previously observed in order to address the source(s) of these massive
sedimentary releases. Within these regions, geophysical soundings were
used to detect gas seeps that could indicate strong CH4 release by ebullition.
Most sampling took place close to seep sites, while some samples were
retrieved at background locations with lower CH4 concentrations for
comparison (sample details in Supplementary Data 2–5).
Seep detection
The seeps were detected with a scientiﬁc 200 kHz single-beamechosounder,
Simrad EK15. The beam swath was 26°, resulting in a swath diameter of
~20 m and a sounding area of ~300 m2 at ~20 m water depth. The acoustic
survey was conducted at ~11 km h−1. The presence of rising bubbles was
identiﬁed by observing an increased acoustic signal intensity in the water
column (Supplementary Note 3). This type of echosounder data was gen-
erated for the expeditions in 2018, 2019, and 2020. This seep detection
technique has been described elsewhere4,5,87.

Isotopic endmembers of putative subsea methane sources
Three main endmembers were considered for the old (>48 ky BP) CH4 that
wasfound. Theseare:subsea permafrost-associated methane(SPAM),fossil
primary microbial (FOPRIM) gas, and fossil thermogenic and secondary
microbial (FOTSEM) gas (Fig. 1). Full explanations of the endmembers, the
isotopic endmember database, and the resulting constraints on the isotopic
source endmember values can be found in Supplementary Methods 1.2 and
Supplementary Data 1. Isotopic data from 158 articles were included in the
isotopic endmember database. In total, 642, 484, and 2394 isotopic samples
were incorporated in the database to construct the isotopic endmember
values for, respectively, SPAM, FOPRIM gas, and FOTSEM gas.
In short, SPAM covers all forms of microbial CH4 (free gas or hydrate)
formed from OM in the upper subsea permafrost region before/during
freezing and formed upon subsea permafrost thawing. Importantly, this
category thus also encompasses CH4 formed from relatively fresh OM
accumulated during the past several million years and trapped by perma-
frost after formation, i.e., a preformed CH4 pool. As SPAM represents
primary microbiallyformed CH4, the constraineddual-stableisotope values
of this endmember are low (Supplementary Methods 1.2.4 and Supple-
mentary Data 1). The endmember SPAM is situated in the subsea perma-
frost layer (Fig. 1).
ThenaturalgasesFOTSEMandFOPRIMarefossilgasesformedbelow
permafrost by either catagenesis or primary/secondary microbial processes.
The endmember FOPRIM encompasses primary microbial gas formed by
OM degradation at temperatures up to 60–80 °C90–92, using older OM than
SPAM that can be up to several hundred million years old. It is typically
formed up to depths of ~1–3 km91–93. The endmember FOTSEM encom-
passes CH4 formed by catagenesis in even deeper sedimentary basins at a
pressure of 300–1500 bar and a temperature of 50–200 °C90,94. Furthermore,
it encompasses secondary microbial CH4 formed by the biodegradation of
thermogenic petroleum, especially oil, in relatively cold (<70–90 °C)
reservoirs95. The constrained stable carbon isotope value of the endmember
FOPRIM gas is much more negative than that of FOTSEM gas due to the
primary microbial origin of FOPRIM gas (Supplementary Meth-
ods 1.2.1–1.2.3 and Supplementary Data 1). Both FOPRIM and FOTSEM
gases can be present at the location of formation, accumulate in another
reservoir, or get trapped as CH4 hydrate or free gas in or below subsea
permafrost. While isotopic compositions alone do not allow for further
distinction between these compartments, the endmembers FOPRIM and
FOTSEM effectively represent ancient preformed CH₄ pools. The end-
members FOPRIM and FOTSEM gases are indicated with yellow and red,
respectively, in Fig. 1.
The constrained isotopic endmember values for δ13CCH4 are
−43 ± 7‰, −67 ± 5‰, and −74 ± 10‰ for, respectively, FOTSEM,
FOPRIM, and SPAM. The constrained isotopic endmember values for
δ2HCH4 are −196 ± 31‰, −221 ± 38‰, and −322 ± 44‰ for, respectively,
FOTSEM, FOPRIM, and SPAM (database containing individual isotopic
data in Supplementary Data 1).
Statistical source apportionment of the released methane
The isotopic measurements of dissolved CH4 were extrapolated to the
source signatures of the CH4 being released to the water column using
Keeling plots. To obtain the Keeling plots, a linear regression model was
ﬁtted to the measured isotopic values versus the inverse CH4 concentration
using the least squares method. Keeling plots3,96 are applicable for source
determination when a distinct single source or well-mixed group of sources
mixes to different extents into relatively constant background material. If a
mixing scenario is valid, the y-intercept of the Keeling plot gives the isotopic
signature of the source(s). A prerequisite for using Keeling plots is that
fractionating processes, like CH4 oxidation, do not dominate the observed
isotopicchangesinthemixingmedium,whichisseawaterinthiscase.Inour
study, the Keeling plot-extrapolated values (y-intercepts) were combined
withtheconstrainedendmemberdata inaBayesianMCMCapproach56,57 to
derive the probability density functions of the fractions of each source
category. A Bayesian MCMC approach was used to account for the isotopic
uncertainty of the identiﬁed endmembers and to obtain the probability
density functions of the source fractions, thereby quantifying the uncer-
tainties associated with these results. A complete description of this pro-
cedure can be found in Supplementary Methods 1.3.
